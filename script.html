Unifying Global and Local Features for Precise Action Spotting

1. Table of Content and Task Overview (Slides 1 & 2)

Estimated time: 2 minutes

Good morning, everyone, and thank you for being here. Today, I am excited to present my work titled Unifying Global and Local Features, or UGL for short. We are introducing a modern approach to sports video analysis.

To give you a quick overview of our task, we are focusing on the domain of action spotting within sports videos—specifically looking at complex, fast-paced sports like soccer, diving, and gymnastics. Unlike traditional action recognition, where the goal might be to classify a long temporal segment, our goal is much more precise. We want to detect the exact, precise timestamps of events, not long temporal segments. We are looking for the singular moment—the exact frame—when a specific action occurs.

Why is this important? The real-world applications are vast. This technology is the backbone for automatic highlight generation, deep video analytics and broadcasting automation, and downstream temporal retrieval. It allows broadcasters to instantly pull up key moments without manually scrubbing through hours of footage.

2. Motivation for UGL (Slide 3)

Estimated time: 3 minutes

Let's move on to the motivation for developing UGL. If we look at standard action spotting pipelines today, we notice a common limitation: they rely almost entirely on global frame features only. They process the video frame as a whole, treating the scene essentially as black boxes. The problem with this approach is that the models are completely missing small but critical entities in the scene. In a soccer match, for instance, the difference between a foul and a regular play might depend entirely on a tiny object like the ball, a referee's whistle, or a red or yellow card.

When we review the current literature, we see two main directions, both with significant drawbacks. First, there are two-phase approaches. These use heavy 3D backbones and multiple extractors. While accurate, they are incredibly computationally expensive and complex to deploy. Second, we have end-to-end methods, such as E2E-Spot. These are lighter but still global-only—they lack the fine-grained understanding of local entities. Our motivation with UGL was to bridge this gap: to build a model that is lightweight like an end-to-end method, but highly perceptive of critical local details.

3. Global Environment Feature (Slides 4 & 5)

Estimated time: 3 minutes

Let's dive into the UGL Architecture, starting with how we capture the overall scene. We call this the Global Environment Feature. For our backbone, we chose RegNet-Y. This is an efficient 2D CNN design that gives us excellent spatial feature extraction without the immense overhead of a 3D CNN.

However, since we are working with video, we still need to understand how the scene changes over time. For our Temporal Integration, we incorporate the Gate-Shift Module, or GSM. What the GSM does is remarkably efficient: it shifts feature maps forward and backward along the temporal axis. This allows neighboring frames to naturally share information with one another. It serves as a highly effective, lightweight alternative to 3D CNNs.

The output of this branch is a single global environment feature vector per frame. This vector successfully captures spatial and short-term temporal context of the players' immediate movements.

4. Local Scene Entities with GLIP (Slide 6)

Estimated time: 4 minutes

But as I mentioned earlier, the global environment is only half the story. We also need to understand the specifics. This brings us to the Local Scene Entities branch. Why do we need this? Because global features alone often ignore small but crucial entities, especially under severe class imbalance.

To solve this, we leverage GLIP, which stands for Grounded Language-Image Pre-training. The beauty of GLIP is that it acts as a robust, training-free detector. The input to GLIP is twofold: the raw frame image, and a specific text prompt listing sports entities—for example, we feed it the words "ball, yellow card, red card, referee".

Internally, GLIP's mechanism is fascinating. A Language encoder processes the text using BERT. Meanwhile, a Visual encoder produces image proposals using a Swin backbone. Finally, a Cross-modal multi-head attention aligns the proposals with the textual entities, effectively locating the key objects in the scene based purely on our text prompt.

5. Adaptive Attention Mechanism (Slide 7)

Estimated time: 2 minutes

Now, GLIP might find many objects, but we have a new problem: not all detected entities are equally relevant for the current action. A referee standing idle on the sideline is less important than a referee actively pulling out a red card.

To filter this information, we designed the Adaptive Attention Mechanism, or AAM. The AAM solution takes both the local entity features and our global environment feature, and applies a two-step refinement. First, it uses Hard attention to actively select the most promising entities. Second, it applies Soft self-attention to fuse them into a single compact representation.

The output of this module is the Local relevant scene entities feature. This feature perfectly emphasizes objects crucial for the action taking place.

6. Fusion of Global and Local (Slides 8 & 9)

Estimated time: 2 minutes

With our global context and our refined local details in hand, we reach the Fusion stage. Our goal here is to create a unified understanding of the frame.

The process is straightforward but powerful. First, we Concatenate the Global environment feature and the Local relevant entities feature. Once combined, we Apply a Self-attention layer to model interactions between the environment and the objects, followed by Average pooling for compactness.

The final Output is a Unified entities and environment representation per frame. This representation is the core strength of UGL—it explicitly captures both background context and key object semantics simultaneously.

7. Long-Term Temporal Reasoning (LTR) (Slides 10 & 11)

Estimated time: 2 minutes

Having built this rich per-frame representation, we now need to look at the longer-term sequence of events to make our final prediction. We do this in our Long-Term Temporal Reasoning module, or LTR.

For our Temporal Modeling, we take the sequence of fused frame features for a T-frame snippet as our input. We feed this sequence into our model, which is a 1-layer bidirectional GRU. The bidirectional nature of the GRU is essential here, as it effectively captures semantic relationships of actions across time.

Finally, for our Prediction, we generate per-frame class scores via a fully connected layer and softmax, giving us our precise action timestamps.

8. Benchmark Datasets (Slide 12)

Estimated time: 1 minute

To prove the effectiveness of UGL, we rigorously tested it across three distinct benchmark datasets representing different sports domains.

The most significant is SoccerNet-v2. This is a massive dataset containing ~550 matches, roughly ~764 hours of video, and >300k labeled spots. It is incredibly challenging due to strong clutter and camera changes.

We also evaluated on FineDiving, which contains 3,000 diving clips focusing on complex phase transitions (entry, twist, etc.), and FineGym, which offers 5,374 gym performances across 32 spotting classes that have been converted from action segments to timestamps.

9. Quantitative Results (Slide 13)

Estimated time: 1.5 minutes

Our quantitative results clearly demonstrate the superiority of the UGL architecture. Let's look at the SoccerNet-v2 challenge.

UGL achieves a Tight Average mAP, or T-mAP, of 69.38%. This establishes the current SOTA on this highly competitive benchmark. When we compare this to the previous SOTA, E2E-Spot, which achieved 66.73%, we achieve a massive performance gap of -2.65% for them, or +2.65% for us. We even outperform the leading two-phase approach by Soares by 1.57%.

We see similar success on our other datasets. On FineDiving, UGL shows a +2.4% improvement in mAP over E2E-Spot, and on FineGym, we see a +1.3% improvement. These consistent gains validate our approach.

10. Conclusion (Slides 14 & 15)

Estimated time: 1.5 minutes

To conclude, our work demonstrates that unifying Global + Local entity features produces highly precise, interpretable per-frame representations.

This architecture achieved the top result on SoccerNet-v2: 69.38% T-mAP. Furthermore, combining our unified features with a GRU + Focal Loss handles temporal reasoning and class imbalance effectively.

Appendix Backup Notes (Slides 16-18)

Optional reading if asked during Q&A
  • Gate-Shift Module (GSM): Used to add short-term temporal context without heavy 3D CNNs by shifting feature maps.
  • RoIAlign in GLIP: Turns detected boxes of varying sizes (like a small ball vs. a large referee) into a fixed-size feature map for fair comparison.
  • AAM specifics: Reiterate that Hard attention selects promising entities from GLIP, while Soft self-attention fuses them to emphasize only what matters for spotting.
